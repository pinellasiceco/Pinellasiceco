Know Your Market
Pinellas County — who you're selling to
▼
The Pinellas Restaurant Owner

Pinellas County's median age is 49 — older than the state average. The restaurant owners you'll meet are predominantly established operators. Many have owned their business for 10–20 years. They've been pitched by dozens of vendors and have a sharp instinct for salespeople. They buy from people they trust, not from people who pitch them.

The county's economy is anchored in tourism and hospitality. Clearwater Beach, St. Pete Beach, and downtown St. Pete generate millions of visitor days per year. High foot traffic means high ice demand and machines that run harder and get dirtier faster. Florida heat and humidity mean bacteria and scale grow faster here than almost anywhere else in the country.

⚡ Seasonal insight: June–September, Pinellas waterfront restaurants run machines at 90%+ capacity. That's when neglected machines fail and inspections spike. The best window to close these accounts is March–May, before summer creates urgency. Sell prevention before the pain arrives.
Cultural Landscape — Who You'll Meet

Pinellas is 71% White, 11% Hispanic, 9% Black. The adjacent Hillsborough market is 25% Hispanic with the highest concentration of Cuban and Puerto Rican ownership in the region. As your territory grows, these segments grow.

Cuban and Hispanic-owned restaurants are among your best prospects — family-operated, independent, owner usually present, community-networked. The concept of confianza (earned trust) governs decision-making. Do not rush. Do not use pressure. Ask about their restaurant before you mention your service. A warm referral from a trusted community contact will outperform 20 cold walk-ins in this segment.

Long-established independents (10+ years) are your most stable recurring clients. They've seen operators lose their license. They care about compliance. They respond to expertise and reliability over price. Earn trust once and they become advocates.

Newer independents (under 5 years) are more price-sensitive but also more open. They haven't built their full vendor roster and are often genuinely unaware of what professional ice machine maintenance looks like.

The Walk-In Playbook
Car to close — every step
▼
Before You Walk In — 60-Second Car Ritual Challenger
Do this every single time. No exceptions.
  1. 1
    Open app. Read their score and priority. Note any inspection history.
  2. 2
    Pick your angle in 10 seconds:
    CALLBACK — compliance urgency HOT — go straight to ATP demo WARM — prevention angle
  3. 3
    Say out loud: "I have something valuable to offer. I'm not asking for a favor."
  4. 4
    Get out of the car.
Best Windows to Walk In
★ Tue–Thu 2:00–4:30pm Best universal window. Prep done, pre-dinner calm.
Wed–Thu 2:00–3:30pm Owner just ran lunch. Reflective mindset. Receptive.
Tue–Thu 10:00–11:30am Good for neighborhood spots. Owner doing morning admin.
Sat 9:00–11:00am Cuban/family owners doing setup. Unhurried before rush.
Fri afternoon, Sun, 12–2pm, before 10am Avoid. Owner unavailable or in service mode.
Step 1 — At the Host Stand

Walk in with quiet confidence. You look like a technician making a courtesy stop — not a salesperson. That distinction matters more than you think in a market full of vendors owners have learned to avoid.

At host stand
"Hi, I'm looking for the owner or general manager — is [name] available?"

If they ask what it's about: "I'm a commercial ice machine sanitation tech — I'm in the area and wanted to stop by for two minutes."

Never say "sales call." Never say "tell you about our services."

Step 2 — The Opening Sandler

The first sentence determines everything. Sandler principle: open with their world, not yours. Create a question in their mind before you answer anything.

The Sandler opener
"I only need two minutes. Can I ask you one question? When was the last time someone actually opened your ice machine and documented what they found?"

This is a pain funnel entry. They either say "recently" — ask who and how often, which reveals the gap — or they say "I'm not sure," which means you've already found the pain.

Listen. Do not fill silence. What they say next tells you more about how to close them than anything you'll say in response.

Step 3 — The Machine Offer Challenger
The pivot
"Mind if I take a look right now? Two minutes. I'll tell you exactly what an inspector would flag — no charge, no obligation."

When you open the machine, say nothing for a moment. Let them look. If there's scale or biofilm visible, let the silence work. You don't need to editorialize what they can see with their own eyes.

Step 4 — ATP Demo (see Section 03)

Hand them the meter. Say nothing while it reads. The number closes.

Step 5 — The Offer SPIN

After the reading, use SPIN's Implication + Need-Payoff before presenting price:

Implication (SPIN)
"If an inspector ran that same test during a surprise visit, what would that mean for your license?"

Let them answer. Then:

Need-Payoff (SPIN)
"If I could give you a compliance report showing a 90%+ reduction in that reading — documented, dated, available to show any inspector — how would that change things?"

Then present the $228 bundled service. See Section 14 (Track A) for the full value stack.

Stop. Wait for them to respond.

When the Owner Isn't There
Leave-behind handoff
"Can you make sure [name] sees this? Tell them the ice machine tech stopped by."

A compliance report on a manager's desk has a shelf life. A business card gets thrown away.

The Phone Call Playbook
Pre-call to close — every step
▼
The Phone vs Walk-In Difference

A phone call and a walk-in are fundamentally different sales events. On a walk-in, the ATP demo closes. On a phone call, you have 5–10 seconds before they form an opinion and 30 seconds before they decide to end it. There is no meter, no visual, no machine to point at. You are selling one thing and one thing only: the walk-in appointment.

Never try to close a monthly contract on a cold call. Never pitch pricing on a cold call. The close on a phone call is a scheduled visit. That's it.

🧠 The mindset shift: Walk-in goal = close the service. Phone call goal = earn the walk-in. Every script below is written around that distinction.
Pre-Call Ritual — 30 Seconds Challenger
Before every call. Non-negotiable.
  1. 1
    Open their card. Read: priority score, city, inspection history, any prior contact notes.
  2. 2
    Decide your angle:
    CALLBACK — compliance urgency, re-inspection HOT — recent violation, time-sensitive WARM — prevention, before season
  3. 3
    Have their name ready. Not "the owner" — their actual name if you have it.
  4. 4
    Stand up if you can. Your voice carries differently standing. Not a joke.
Step 1 — Getting Past the Gatekeeper

The person who answers is almost never the owner. Your goal is not to pitch them. Your goal is to get them to put the owner on — or get you a name and a callback time.

Standard opener to gatekeeper
"Hi, is [owner name] in? This is [your name] — I'm calling about their health inspection compliance."

The phrase "health inspection compliance" is not a trick. It is accurate. It creates appropriate urgency without being deceptive. Most gatekeepers will put you through or get the owner.

If they ask what company
"Pinellas Ice Co — we do commercial ice machine sanitation and compliance documentation for restaurants in [city]."
If owner isn't available — get the info
"No problem — can I ask your owner's name? And is there a better time to reach them directly — morning or afternoon?"

Never leave a generic voicemail with a gatekeeper. Get the owner's name. Get a callback window. Log it and call back at the specific time they give you.

Step 2 — The Opener (5 Seconds to Earn 30 More) Challenger

Most vendor calls start with: "Hi, I wanted to tell you about our services." Owners hear this 10 times a week and their brain immediately categorizes you as a sales call to end.

Your opener leads with something specific and true about their business — not about yours. That's the Challenger principle on a phone call: teach first, pitch never.

CALLBACK priority opener
"Hi [name], this is [your name] from Pinellas Ice Co. Quick question — I work with restaurants in [city] and I noticed your location had a callback inspection. Has that been fully resolved with documentation?"
HOT priority opener
"Hi [name], this is [your name] from Pinellas Ice Co. I study the FL DBPR inspection data for restaurants in [city] — your location came up on my radar this week. Do you have 60 seconds?"
WARM priority / general opener
"Hi [name], this is [your name] from Pinellas Ice Co. I do commercial ice machine sanitation for restaurants in [city]. Quick question — when was the last time someone actually opened your ice machine and documented what they found?"
⚡ The last question is the same Sandler opener as the walk-in. It works on phone too because it's specific and creates genuine curiosity. Most owners don't know the answer and that moment of uncertainty is your window.
Step 3 — The 30-Second Hook Sandler

If they're still on the line after your opener, they're curious. Don't waste it with features. Go straight to pain discovery. You have 30 seconds.

After they respond (any response)
"Most restaurants I work with haven't had their machine properly documented — not because they're doing anything wrong, it's just not something most vendors actually provide. The issue is that health inspectors are starting to specifically test the internal water contact surfaces. Do you know if you'd have documentation showing your readings if they asked?"

Then stop. Let them respond. This question creates real anxiety without being manipulative — because it's true and because most of them genuinely don't have that documentation.

If they say they have service already
"Good — do they give you a compliance report with ATP readings after every visit? Something you could actually show an inspector?"

Most won't have this. The gap between "we have service" and "we have documentation" is your entire competitive advantage on a phone call.

Step 4 — The One Ask SPIN

After the hook, you have earned the right to ask for one thing. Ask for the walk-in. Not the sale. Not the monthly contract. The visit.

The appointment ask — standard
"Here's what I'd like to do. I'm in [city] on [Tuesday/Thursday]. Can I stop by for 10 minutes — I'll run a quick ATP test on your machine and show you exactly where you stand. No charge, no commitment."
The appointment ask — CALLBACK priority
"Given that you've had a callback inspection, I'd like to come by before your re-inspection window closes. I can get you documented in one visit. When's the best time this week?"
If they hesitate on time
"It's genuinely 10 minutes. I'm not going to pitch you in your kitchen — I'm going to show you a number on a meter and let you decide. Tuesday at 2 or Thursday at 3 — either work for you?"

The two-option close ("Tuesday at 2 or Thursday at 3") eliminates the binary yes/no. They're choosing a time, not deciding whether to let you in. Small but meaningful difference.

Step 5 — Confirming the Appointment

When they agree to a time, do three things before you hang up:

  1. 1
    Confirm the specific day and time out loud: "Perfect — so Tuesday the 22nd around 2pm. I'll be there."
  2. 2
    Get their cell if you don't have it: "Best number if I need to reach you directly?"
  3. 3
    Set their expectation: "I'll come in, run the test, show you the results, and you can ask me anything. 10 minutes max."
✓ Log the appointment immediately in the app before you make your next call. Set a reminder for the day before to confirm. Owners who agreed on a Tuesday have usually forgotten by Monday.
The Voicemail Script — Exact Words

You will hit voicemail constantly. Most people leave a generic message that gets deleted immediately. Yours should do one thing: make them curious enough to call back or recognize your name when you walk in. See Section 21 for the complete voicemail guide.

Standard voicemail (30 seconds max)
"Hi [name], this is [your name] from Pinellas Ice Co — number is [number]. I study the inspection data for restaurants in [city] and your location came up this week. Nothing urgent, just had a quick question about your ice machine compliance documentation. Call me back when you get a chance, or I'll try you again [day]. Thanks."

Three voicemail rules:

  1. 1
    Always leave your number slowly and clearly — twice if needed. They're usually walking and can't write it down the first time.
  2. 2
    Always say when you'll call back. "I'll try you Thursday" makes the next call a continuation, not a cold call.
  3. 3
    Never leave more than one voicemail per week. Two voicemails in 48 hours makes you a pest. One well-placed voicemail makes you persistent.
Phone-Specific Objections Sandler
🔴 "We're not interested" (said within 10 seconds)
This is a reflex, not a decision. They haven't heard enough to be genuinely uninterested. Don't accept it at face value on a cold call.

"Completely understand — can I ask one quick question before I let you go? When was the last time someone actually tested your ice machine's water contact surfaces with an ATP meter?"

If they engage: you're back in. If they repeat "not interested": "No problem. Can I stop by briefly when I'm in [city] next week? 5 minutes." A walk-in is easier to say yes to than a phone commitment.
✓ Turn the call rejection into a walk-in attempt.
🔴 "Send me some information / email me"
Classic brush-off. An email will never close this. A walk-in might.

"I can do that — what's the best email?" (Get it. Log it.) "I'll send that over today. And I'll be honest with you — the most useful thing isn't an email, it's a 10-minute test I can run right at your machine. I'm in [city] this Thursday — would it be okay if I just stopped by while I'm in the area?"

Send a clean one-page summary with a before/after ATP reading. Then follow up Thursday regardless.
✓ Get the email AND push for the walk-in. The email alone is a dead end.
🔴 "We already have someone for that"
Identical to the walk-in version but harder to probe on the phone.

"Good to know — do they provide ATP documentation after each visit? A compliance report you could show an inspector?"

If no: "That's actually the gap most services have. When I stop by I can show you what that documentation looks like — if your current service is covering it, great. If not, at least you'll know. I'm in [city] Thursday."

If yes: "Excellent — sounds like you're well covered. I'll take you off my list." Respect it. Move on.
✓ Probe the documentation gap. If they have it, respect it and move on.
🔴 "How did you get my number?"
Be completely honest. This never needs to be awkward.

"Your restaurant is listed in the Florida DBPR food service database — it's public record. I use the inspection history to prioritize which restaurants to reach out to. Yours came up because of [your inspection activity / your location / your volume profile]."

This is the truth and it's genuinely impressive. You know their inspection history before they know your name. That's a Challenger move delivered with complete transparency.
✓ Transparency here builds credibility. Never be evasive about how you found them.
🔴 "We're too busy right now / call me back next month"
This is often genuine, especially near meal service. Don't fight it.

"Of course — when's a better time to reach you? Morning or afternoon, and which day of the week is usually lighter for you?"

Get a specific time. Log it. Call at that exact time. If they said "call Tuesday morning," call Tuesday at 10am. That specificity is how you convert a brush-off into a booked appointment.
✓ Get a specific callback time and honor it exactly.
Cuban / Hispanic Owner Phone Approach

The confianza dynamic that applies on walk-ins applies even more acutely on the phone — because you have no physical presence to establish credibility. A cold call from an unknown number to a Cuban family restaurant owner is a hard start.

Two adaptations:

  1. 1
    Reference the neighborhood, not the service. "I work with a few restaurants on [specific street or area in their city]" creates local credibility immediately. If you have a client nearby: "I work with [restaurant name] down on [street] — they introduced me to a few other owners in the area."
  2. 2
    The call is the warm-up, the walk-in is the sale. Don't try to close anything on the phone. The goal is simply to make your name familiar before you appear at their door. "I wanted to introduce myself before stopping by — I'll be in [city] this Thursday." That's a much softer ask and significantly more effective with this segment.
If you speak any Spanish: opening with "Buenos días, ¿está el dueño?" before switching to English signals cultural awareness and earns 30 seconds of goodwill that a cold English opener won't.

The ATP Pitch Protocol
Your single most powerful selling tool
▼
Why This Closes Challenger

The ATP test is a textbook Challenger Sale. You're showing them something true about their own business they couldn't see. Numbers remove opinion from the conversation. An owner can argue with your words. They cannot argue with 847 RLU on a meter they're holding in their own hands.

⚠ Never talk while the meter is reading. The silence is intentional. The moment they see the number is your highest-leverage selling moment. Don't dilute it with words.
Pre-Clean Swab Locations — Maximum Contamination
  1. 1
    Water trough interior — biofilm accumulates here constantly. Wet, dark, never cleaned by staff. Almost always the highest reading. Z-pattern, firm pressure.
  2. 2
    Underside of water curtain — dark, permanently wet, invisible to staff. Biofilm starts here. Swab the underside edge.
  3. 3
    Ice scoop handle — touched hundreds of times daily. Cross-contamination. Looks clean. Reads high.
  4. 4
    Inside bin near drain — ice runoff pools. Always elevated.
  5. 5
    Float valve housing — scale and organic material concentrate here. Rarely cleaned.
The Pitch Move — Execute Exactly
  1. 1
    Open the front panel.
  2. 2
    Say nothing. Let them look first.
  3. 3
    Pull out Ultrasnap. Snap tube. "Let me show you something."
  4. 4
    Swab trough, Z-pattern, firm pressure. About 4 seconds.
  5. 5
    Insert swab into Ensure v2. Hand the meter to the owner. Say nothing.
  6. 6
    15 seconds. Let them watch the countdown. Let them read the number themselves.
  7. 7
    Pause 2 full seconds after they read it. Then: "Anything over 30 is a potential health inspection citation. Pass is 10 or below."

Objection Handler
Every pushback, exact response
▼
The Core Principle Sandler

An objection is not a no. It's a request for more information, delivered poorly. Every objection has a root fear. Address the fear, not the surface statement. Sandler A-R-C: Acknowledge → Reframe → Close. Never argue. Never defend. Redirect.

Common Objections
🔴 "We clean it ourselves."
Root fear: Being told they're doing something wrong. Ego investment.

"I'm sure you do — running the clean cycle and wiping down the exterior is exactly the right habit. What I do is different. I open the water system, pull the components, descale the evaporator, and test the contamination levels with a meter before and after. That's the part inspectors actually look at — and you can't get to it without disassembly." Then: "Can I run a 30-second test right now? You'll see exactly where you stand — no charge."
✓ The meter settles it without argument.
🔴 "We already have someone."
Root fear: Switching vendors feels like work. Stability preference.

"Good — how often do they come out? And do they give you a written report with ATP readings you can show an inspector?" Pause. If no: "That's the gap. Anyone can run a cleaning service. We prove it was done to a standard that protects your license." If yes: "Excellent. Would you be open to a comparison visit just to benchmark? A second set of eyes on a food safety item never hurts."
✓ "Most of our clients had someone before us. The difference is the documentation."
🔴 "That's too expensive / we can't afford it."
Root fear: Adding a line item. Budget pressure is real in restaurants.

"Most services around here charge $250 to $300 for a single visit. Crystal Ice is $295. We bundle the initial service and first month for $228 — that gets your machine certified and starts the documentation record. After that, $129 a month, every 90 days." Then: "What would a health inspection citation cost you — in fines, reinspection fees, and the Yelp review that follows?"
✓ "$228 today, everything included. One decision."
🔴 "My landlord handles that."
Root fear: Not my problem to solve.

"Do they have documentation of the last service — a compliance report you could show an inspector?" (Almost always: no.) "The challenge is that when the inspector writes a citation, it goes on your license, not your landlord's. The responsibility is yours as the licensee." Offer to handle the service directly and send the documentation to building management. You're solving their problem, not creating one.
✓ "I handle it, you hand the report to your landlord. Everybody's covered."
🔴 "Let me think about it."
Root fear: Missing information — or no pain felt yet. Sandler: no pain, no sale.

Never hand a card and leave. "Of course — what specifically would be most helpful to know before you decide?" Listen. If they can't articulate it, the real objection hasn't surfaced. "Most people want to see the documentation before committing. Can I show you a before-and-after report from a job I did nearby this week?"
✓ "I'm back in this area Thursday. Can I stop by when [owner] is in?" Always leave with a defined next step.
🔴 "We just passed our last inspection."
Root fear: If it ain't broke, don't fix it.

"That's great — when was it?" Then: "Inspectors don't always swab inside the unit — they're often checking paperwork and visible surfaces. Would you like to see what the water contact surfaces look like right now?" The ATP demo still closes this. A passing inspection and a clean machine are two different things.
✓ "Our goal isn't just passing. It's never having that conversation with an inspector at all."
🔴 "Your competitor is cheaper."
Root fear: Overpaying. Comparison shopping.

"That's possible. Do they provide ATP documentation with before-and-after readings? And a compliance report for your records?" Most cheaper services don't. "The price difference is the documentation." Then give context: "A single professional cleaning in this market typically runs $250–300. Our $228 gets you the service and your first month enrolled — you're all-in and documented from day one."
✓ "I'm not the cheapest. I'm the one you call when an inspector walks in."
🔴 "I need to talk to my partner / corporate."
Respect this one. Don't push a close they don't have authority to make.

"Totally understand — who else is involved in this decision?" Get the name. "What would be most helpful to leave with you for that conversation?" Leave excellent materials. "Would it be easier if I came back when [name] is here, or would it help to send information directly to them?" Never leave without a defined next step.
✓ Get a specific date, name, or contact method. A vague "they'll call you" is a dead end.
🟢 "What's actually included?"
Buying signal. Answer completely and confidently.

"$228 covers your initial deep clean and ATP certification — full disassembly, before-and-after readings, timestamped photos, certification sticker, and printed compliance report. That also starts your first month on the quarterly plan. After that, $129 a month — we're back every 90 days, same documentation every visit. No contract."
✓ This answer closes more often than not. Have your value stack card ready.

The Six Personas You'll Meet
🏖 The Beach / Tourist Zone Operator
Waterfront or tourist corridor location, high volume, seasonal business. Often a manager, not the owner.
Angle: Summer urgency. "Your machine runs at maximum capacity June through September — that's when machines fail and inspections spike." If owner isn't there, qualify the manager's authority and get the owner's name. Sell prevention before the summer pain arrives.
🇨🇺 The Cuban / Hispanic Family Owner
Family-operated, owner present most hours, pride in their establishment. Community-connected. Warm but cautious with new vendors.
Angle: Relationship first — always. Ask about their restaurant before mentioning your service. How long have they been open? What's their specialty? Show genuine interest. Confianza — earned trust — must come before the sale. Never rush. The ATP demo still closes, but the relationship opens the door. One referral in this community is worth 20 cold walk-ins. Ask explicitly: "Do you know other restaurant owners nearby who might benefit from this?"
🏠 The Long-Established Neighborhood Owner (10+ years)
Same location for years, loyal customer base, has been burned by vendors before, skeptical baseline.
Angle: Expertise and reliability. They're not buying a cleaning service — they're buying a vendor they can trust. Lead with your inspection intelligence: "I study FL DBPR data for every restaurant in Pinellas — I know what inspectors are flagging in your neighborhood right now." That's differentiated knowledge they've never heard from a vendor. Undercommit and overdeliver on the first visit.
🌟 The Downtown St. Pete Independent
Trendy concept, image-conscious, active on social media, younger or younger-minded owner. Google reviews matter.
Angle: Brand protection. "A health inspection citation in Florida is public record — it shows up on Google, Yelp, and the county website within days." Your compliance report is brand insurance. Lead with the documentation story, not the cleaning story.
💼 The Decisive Owner — wants it handled
Quick decisions, asks "what do you recommend," doesn't haggle.
Angle: Give them a clear recommendation, not options. "Based on what I'm seeing, let's do the service today — $228, you're certified and on the quarterly plan from here." Don't offer three tiers. Give them the answer.
🔍 The Skeptic — needs proof before anything
Arms crossed, lots of questions, references past vendors who disappointed them.
Angle: Evidence only. Let the ATP number do the work. Show before-and-after photos and a sample compliance report. "Let me earn your trust with one visit before we talk about anything ongoing." Never close hard with a skeptic — they close themselves when the proof is undeniable.

Using inspection intelligence before you walk in

What You Know Before You Knock Challenger

You have 6 years of FL DBPR inspection history on every prospect. No competitor walking in off the street has this. This is your commercial insight — the Challenger principle. You know their compliance history before they know your name.

⚠ Do not reveal specific violation dates or case numbers. Frame it as your specialty: "I work specifically with restaurants that have had inspection activity" — not "I saw you had a violation on [date]."

The Brush-Off
One more line · leave something real · get permission to return
▼
Recognize It — and What It Actually Means

A brush-off is a bandwidth problem, not an information problem. They are not saying "I've evaluated your service and concluded it has no value." They are saying "I don't have the cognitive space for this right now." Do not respond to the words. Respond to the actual situation.

Brush-off sounds like:
"We're good thanks" / "Not interested" / "We already have someone"
"Send me some info" / "My boss isn't here" / "Not a good time"

Your goal when you hit a brush-off: one more line, a leave-behind, and permission to return. That's it. Three things. Do not try to close through a brush-off — it makes it worse every time.

The One-More-Line Rule

After the brush-off, you get exactly one more line. Make it count. It should create a small question in their mind — not a pitch, not a close, just enough to leave them thinking about one specific thing.

Standard one more line
"Totally understand. Last thing I'll say — the ATP test is free and takes two minutes. If your machine is clean, great — you'll never hear from me again. If it's not, you'll want to know before an inspector finds it."

Then stop. Hand them the leave-behind and move to the permission ask. More words after this make it worse, not better.

The Leave-Behind Play

Don't leave a business card. Leave something that has a number on it. Specifically: a printed ATP report from a recent job with the business name blacked out. The pre-clean and post-clean RLU readings should be visible.

Write or stamp on it: "This is what I found. This is what I left. Free test — 2 minutes at your machine."

A before/after report with real numbers is the most credible leave-behind you can give. It is proof of concept in one page. Business cards are discarded. Real data stays on the counter.

If you don't have a printed report yet, the value stack card is your fallback. But build the printed reports into your rotation from your first service onward.

The Permission Ask
After the one more line and leave-behind
"Do you mind if I check back in a month or so?"
Almost nobody says no to this. You just got permission to return — and your next visit is no longer a cold call.

Log it immediately. Set a 30-day reminder. When you return, say: "I stopped by about a month ago — you said it was okay to check back." That sentence changes the entire dynamic of the second visit.

On the Phone Brush-Off
Same principle, one line
"No problem at all — last thing: the ATP test is free and takes two minutes at your machine. If it's clean you'll never hear from me again. Mind if I try back in a few weeks?"

Log it. Set the reminder. Next call.

What you do NOT do: Explain more. Pitch harder. Ask another question. Disagree with their brush-off. More words after the one-more-line make every subsequent visit harder.
Same-Day Text (Optional — Use Sparingly)

If you felt real underlying interest beneath the brush-off, a same-day or next-morning text can occasionally surface it. Use this at most once per contact:

Text script
"Hey — [name], Pinellas Ice Co. Stopped by [restaurant] earlier. I know you were busy — wanted to see if a free ATP test this week makes sense. Two minutes at your location."

One shot. If brushed off again — set 30-day reminder and move on. Two texts is a pest. One text is a follow-through.

Six real objections · standing-in-a-kitchen language · exact responses
▼
How to Use This Section

These are the six objections you will hear most often after presenting the ATP test or the $228 price. Each response is written to be said out loud in a working kitchen — not read from a screen. Know them well enough that you don't have to think about them.

1. "I already have someone who takes care of it."
Your response: "Do you know what your machine scored on their last ATP test?"

[Pause. Wait for their answer.]

If they don't know: "That's the gap. A cleaning service and a documented compliance reading are two different things. My service gives you the number — before and after, every visit, on paper. The test tells you if what you're getting is actually working."

If they say yes and have a number: "Good — they're doing it right. Can I ask who they use?" Respect it. Move on. Log it.
✓ The ATP number is the differentiator. If they can't produce a post-service reading, you've found the gap.
2. "The health department never said anything about my ice machine."
Your response: "Let me show you something." Pull up the DBPR record on your phone and hand it to them.

"This is the public inspection record for your location. Right here — [month, year] — ice machine citation. DBPR posts all of these online. They don't always send a formal notice directly, but the record is there and it's what inspectors pull up when they walk in."

Let them read it. Say nothing while they do. The record is the close on this objection — not your explanation of it.
✓ Don't argue. Show them the record. It speaks for itself.
3. "Can I just do the one-time service without starting the program?"
Response A — if they seem hesitant about committing:
"The $228 stands completely on its own. You get the full deep clean, before-and-after ATP readings, timestamped photos, certification sticker, and a printed compliance report. Nothing else required."

Response B — if they're close to yes and asking about the program specifically:
"The service itself is the same either way. The only things exclusive to enrolled clients are the CleanScore QR record and the certification sticker staying active between visits. Everything from the service — the documentation, the photos, the printed report — those are yours regardless."

Note: Never pressure someone into the program. The service stands alone. If they enroll after the first visit, great. If they experience the documentation quality and come back to the program later, that's also a win.
✓ Confirm the service is real value on its own. The program is additive, not required.
4. "I can't afford another monthly expense."
Your response: "Let me show you what your machine is reading before you decide anything."

[Run the ATP test first. Let the number land.]

After the reading: "This is what you're working with right now. The program is $129 a month — that's one visit every 90 days, fully documented. What does a citation cost? Between the fine, the re-inspection fee, and the days you're dealing with it instead of running your restaurant, most people tell me it's $500 minimum and a week of headache."

If they still can't swing the monthly: "The one-time service is $228 — no ongoing commitment. That gets you certified and documented for your next inspection. We can talk about the program after you've seen what the service looks like."
✓ Run the test before the price conversation. The number reframes the cost every time.
5. "We clean it ourselves."
Your response: "The ATP test will confirm it's working."

[Pause. Don't add anything yet.]

"Two minutes — I show you the reading before I touch anything. If your process is keeping it clean, the number will prove it and you'll have documentation to back it up. If the reading is high, you'll know exactly what your inspectors would find."

[After the test, if reading is high:] Don't say "see, I told you." Say: "This is the water trough interior — requires disassembly to reach. Staff can't get to this without taking the unit apart. It's not a cleaning habit problem, it's a surface access problem."

Let the number carry the conversation. You don't need to be right. The meter is right.
✓ Never tell them they're wrong. Offer to prove them right — and let the number do the work.
6. "Send me some information."
Your response: "Absolutely — what's the best email?"

[Get the email. Log it.]

"I'll send two things: the ATP protocol we use and your DBPR inspection record. The record is public — I just pull it and send it so you have it in one place. I'll follow up in three days — Tuesday work?"

Send within the hour. Subject line: "Your DBPR record + what ATP testing shows — Pinellas Ice Co." Email body: one short paragraph, the DBPR record PDF or link, and a sample ATP compliance report. No price in the email. The email gets you Tuesday's call — Tuesday's call gets you the walk-in.
✓ Get the email, send the DBPR record (not just a brochure), confirm Tuesday. The DBPR record makes the follow-up call non-generic.

Can't Get to the Machine
Play 1: work to get there · Play 2: create a confirmed return
▼
Why This Happens

You will regularly encounter situations where you're standing in the building but can't get to the machine: it's in a locked storage area, the key holder isn't present, the kitchen is mid-service, or the manager doesn't have authority to let you back there. Do not treat this as a failed visit. Treat it as a logistics problem with two plays.

Play 1 — Work to Get There

Before you accept "you can't get there today," try to solve the access problem. Most locked-machine situations have a solution.

  1. 1
    Ask who has access. "Is there anyone here who has a key to the machine room / storage area?" You're looking for one person with a key, not permission from the whole staff.
  2. 2
    Offer to work around service. "I can wait 20 minutes until the rush slows down. The test takes two minutes — I won't be in the way." Patience here converts more "can't right now" situations than any script.
  3. 3
    Get the owner on the phone. "Can you get the owner on the phone for 30 seconds? I just want to show them the reading — they can say yes or no." Most staff will make that call. Most owners will say yes.
Access is the obstacle, not interest. If they were willing to talk to you this long, they're not against the service — they just can't get you to the machine. Solve the access problem first.
Play 2 — The Graceful Exit with a Confirmed Return

If access genuinely isn't happening today, your exit must produce one thing: a specific agreed-upon return time. Not a card. Not "stop by again sometime." A day and a time that you both acknowledge out loud.

The exit
"No problem — I completely understand. Let me leave something for [owner name]."

Hand the leave-behind (printed ATP report from a recent job, business name redacted, with real before/after readings visible).

Confirmation ask
"Can you tell [owner name] I'll call Tuesday morning? Or — is [day / time] a better time to catch them here?"

Get a specific answer. Write it down in front of them. "Tuesday at 10" is a commitment. Log it before you leave the parking lot. Set the reminder. Show up Tuesday at 10 and say: "I spoke with [staff member] last [day] — they said this was a good time to catch [owner name]."

A confirmed return time transforms a failed visit into a booked appointment. The machine will still be there Tuesday. The opportunity is only lost if you leave without a return.

Voicemail
Context only — never selling — three rules, three scripts
▼
The One Job a Voicemail Has

A voicemail does not sell. A voicemail does not close. A voicemail has exactly one job: make your name familiar before your face or your next call appears.

The moment a voicemail tries to pitch a service, explain pricing, or describe the quarterly program, it sounds like every other vendor voicemail and gets deleted. A voicemail that says "I'll be there Thursday at 10" plants an appointment in the listener's mind — whether they call back or not. When you show up Thursday, you are a follow-up.

The Three Rules
  1. 1
    Never mention price. Not $228. Not $129/month. Not "affordable." Not "free intro." No price information in a voicemail ever.
  2. 2
    Never pitch the service. Don't describe what you do in detail. Don't say "I'd love to tell you about our services." Say one specific thing that creates a question in their mind, then stop.
  3. 3
    Always say when you're calling back or stopping by. "I'll try you again Thursday" makes your next call an expected continuation. "Call me when you get a chance" gives them nothing to anticipate.
Script 1 — DBPR Cited (20 seconds max)
Script
"Hey — this is [name] with Pinellas Ice Co, number is [number]. I work with restaurants in Pinellas on ice machine compliance and I noticed in public inspection records that your location had a citation come up. Just had a quick question — nothing urgent. Call me back when you get a chance, or I'll try you again [day]. Thanks."
Script 2 — No Citation
Script
"Hey — [name] with Pinellas Ice Co, number is [number]. I do ice machine compliance work for restaurants in [city] and I'm going to be in the area [day]. Wanted to stop by and show you what your machine reads on an ATP test — same device health inspectors use, takes two minutes. I'll try you again [day] or you can call me back. Thanks."
Script 3 — Follow-Up Voicemail (after a prior attempt)
Script
"Hey [name] — this is [name] from Pinellas Ice Co again. I know you're busy — I'll keep trying until I catch you. I'll give it one more shot [specific day] and if the timing's not right I'll get out of your hair. Number's [number]. Thanks."

This is your final voicemail attempt on a lead. Honest, low-pressure, slightly self-aware. It usually produces a callback or a walk-in response more reliably than a third identical voicemail would.

Voicemail Mechanics
  1. 1
    Say your number slowly, twice. They're usually walking or in the middle of something. The second repetition is what they actually write down.
  2. 2
    30 seconds maximum. Anything longer gets cut off or skimmed. Time yourself.
  3. 3
    One voicemail per week maximum. Two in 48 hours = deleted without listening. One well-timed voicemail = persistent.
  4. 4
    Log it immediately. Set the callback date in the app before your next call. "I'll try Thursday" means nothing if Thursday comes and you've forgotten.

The Power Number
Your real average RLU from Pinellas machines — built from your own data
▼
What It Is

The Power Number is the average pre-clean ATP reading from your own tested machines in Pinellas. It is your most credible single-sentence opening in a Track B approach or cold phone call. "The average machine I test in Pinellas reads [X]" is a real number from your real work, and it hits completely differently than a generic claim about industry contamination averages.

Right now you don't have it. That's fine — it gets built over your first 15 services. Until then, use your most recent actual pre-clean reading: "The last machine I tested in [city] read [X]." That's specific and honest. Don't fabricate a number.

How to Build It

Record every pre-clean and post-clean reading after every service. This data compounds. After 15 readings, calculate your average pre-clean RLU. That's your Power Number. Update it quarterly as your dataset grows.

A real average from your own data outperforms any industry statistic you could cite. It is yours. It is local. It is specific. No other service walking into those restaurants can say that number — because they didn't take those readings.
Why It Compounds

As your dataset grows, the Power Number becomes more powerful, not less. At 15 readings it's credible. At 50 readings it's local market data that no competitor has. At 100 readings it's publishable — you can put it in an email, on a leave-behind, or in a message to DBPR contacts as a legitimate market finding.

Every service you do adds to this. Log the readings.
